AI virtual staging: do you have to disclose it?

AiRE Estate Academy guide · Updated October 28, 2026

In practice, yes. Label the staged photo, keep the original next to it, and never change the house itself.

Three layers of rules

The Code of Ethics. If you are a REALTOR®, Article 12 requires a true picture in your advertising, and Standard of Practice 12-10 says that duty covers Internet content and images and prohibits “misleading images.” It sets no label wording; the duty is not to mislead.

Your MLS. Most of the specific rules live here. NorthstarMLS in Minnesota, for example, asks that any virtually staged, AI-generated or AI-enhanced photo be identified in the caption, on the photo or in the remarks, with an unaltered “before” photo of the same room. Read your own MLS's rules; they differ.

State law. California went furthest. Since January 1, 2026, Business and Professions Code § 10140.8 requires agents who advertise with a digitally altered image to put a statement on or next to it saying it was altered, plus a link or QR code to the original, unaltered photo.

What counts as altered

California's definition is a useful test anywhere. These edits are not “digitally altered” under it: lighting, sharpening, white balance, color correction, angle, straightening, cropping and exposure, as long as they don't change how the property looks. Adding, removing or changing anything is: furniture, fixtures, flooring, paint color, landscaping, the facade, and even what's outside, such as utility poles or the view through a window.

EditLabel it?
Brighten a dark room, straighten the linesUsually no
Add furniture to an empty roomYes, and show the original
Green the lawn, swap the sky, remove a power lineYes, and many MLSs ban it outright
Change paint, floors, counters or wallsDon't. That changes the house a buyer will see.
A buyer who tours the house should recognize every room from the photos. If they wouldn't, the photo is misleading, label or no label.
