Texting real estate leads: the consent rules in plain English

AiRE Estate Academy guide · Updated October 28, 2026

Most texting trouble comes from two things: texting people who never said yes, and texting people after they said stop.

The law: the TCPA

The federal Telephone Consumer Protection Act covers marketing calls and texts. The FCC treats a text as a call under it. The fines are the reason to care: $500 per violation, up to $1,500 if the violation is willful (47 U.S.C. § 227(b)(3)), and each text can count separately.

Get a clear yes first

Automated or bulk marketing texts need the person's prior express written consent. Put plain consent language on your lead forms, and keep a record of who agreed, when and on which form. A lead who filled out a form for something else has not agreed to your texts.

What changed in 2025

The FCC had adopted a “one-to-one consent” rule set to start January 27, 2025, which would have required consent for one seller at a time. On January 24, 2025, the Eleventh Circuit vacated it, and the FCC later removed it from its rules. The prior consent requirements still apply. NAR's legal team put it this way: real estate professionals are not required to get one-to-one consent, but should use best practices.

STOP means stop

Since April 11, 2025, a person can take back consent in any reasonable way, including replying “stop,” “quit,” “cancel,” “unsubscribe,” “revoke,” “opt out” or “end.” You have no more than 10 business days to honor it. Honor it the same day anyway.
