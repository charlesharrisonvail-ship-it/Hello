An assistant that works in a lesson and an assistant that works in your week are different things. The second one has to survive busy Fridays, price changes, a new brokerage disclosure, and you forgetting how you set it up three months from now.

So this last module is about routine. Three parts: a weekly run, a monthly check, and a written record.

The weekly run. Put it on your calendar. Friday, two o'clock, seller updates. Before you run it, gather the inputs: this week's showing export for each listing, any price change paperwork, and any seller messages. Then run it, one listing at a time. And then the step that never gets skipped: read every draft in full. Check the checklist. Answer every missing, stale and agent-decision item yourself. Then you send. The assistant saved you the typing and the assembly. It didn't save you the judgment.

Keep a short list of hand edits. If you fix the same thing by hand three Fridays in a row, that's not you being picky. That's your instructions telling you they need an update.

The monthly check. First Friday of the month. Reread your Voice Profile: does it still sound like you? Rerun the ten-case test log and record the date. Remove reference files for listings that closed or were withdrawn, because last spring's showing log has no business being in the pack. And if you changed the instructions, bump the version number and write one line in the change history: what changed, why, and whether you reran the tests.

That change history sounds like paperwork. It's the thing that saves you in March, when a draft does something odd and you need to know whether it's the tool, the sources, or the line you added in January.

Now, the boundaries, one more time, because routine is when they slip. Your assistant drafts. These things are never its decision: pricing and price changes; anything about what a contract or a law means; whether to disclose something; how to handle a complaint; and anything a seller asks you to say that isn't documented. When those come up, the checklist says agent decision, and you decide, or you bring in your broker or an attorney. A routine makes the easy parts automatic so you have time for the hard parts.

Finally, reuse. You built one assistant, but you also learned a pattern: one job, a voice, a source pack, portable instructions, a test set, and a routine. That pattern works for the next job too.

Take a buyer comparison. Same steps. Job card: turn the facts of two or three homes the buyer toured into a side-by-side comparison, plus a checklist. Inputs: listing facts for each home, the buyer's stated must-haves. Rules: only supplied facts; no school claims; no statements about neighborhoods' people; no "you should buy this one." Test cases: a missing HOA fee, a feature the buyer assumes but the listing doesn't state, a stale listing.

Or listing copy. Job card: turn the listing worksheet into an MLS description and a social caption. Rules: features from the worksheet only; fair-housing language check; no "perfect for families." Tests: a feature the seller wishes were true, a missing room dimension.

I want to be clear: those two aren't built in this course. They're outlines. If you build one, run it through Modules 1 to 6 just like the seller update, including the test set. Don't skip straight to production because the first one worked.

Your exercise: put the weekly run and the monthly check on your calendar, copy the maintenance checklist, and write your Assistant Operating Guide: a one-page summary of what your assistant does, where its files live, how to run it, who decides what, and when it was last tested. If you were out sick, could a colleague run Friday's updates from that page? That's the test.

Then head to the capstone.
