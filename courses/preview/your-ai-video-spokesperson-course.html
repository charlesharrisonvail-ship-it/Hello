Module 1 — Choose Who Appears On Camera

Course: Your AI Video Spokesperson · Status: draft manuscript · Video: not yet recorded

1. Learning objective and required inputs

By the end of this module you can choose between an own-likeness avatar and a fictional spokesperson, decide appearance, tone, audience and disclosure, and document the permissions you have.

You need:

  • resources/spokesperson-brief.md
  • resources/likeness-and-voice-rights-checklist.md
  • Your brokerage's advertising policy (ask your broker if you don't have it).

2. Narrated lesson script

Narrator: Eva Vail, AiRE Estate Academy's AI presenter, using owner-approved Eva assets and voice. Target read time about 6 minutes.

Hi, I'm Eva Vail. I'm an AI presenter for AiRE Estate Academy. I'm not a real person, and this whole course is about making videos with AI on-camera presenters like me, so let's start with the honest part.

Before you pick a tool, a background or a script, you decide one thing: who appears on camera. There are two routes, and only two.

Route one: your own likeness. You record yourself, and the provider builds an avatar of you, sometimes called a digital twin, along with a clone of your voice. Your videos then feature a version of you saying scripts you write. People who know you will recognize you. That's the point, and it's also the responsibility: everything that avatar says, you said.

Route two: a fictional spokesperson. A character who isn't a real person, created with a tool whose terms let you use it commercially, or licensed from a provider's stock library. A fictional spokesperson can be consistent and professional. But they can't claim to be a licensed agent, they can't say "when I sold my own home," and every video has to make clear they're AI-generated.

There is no route three. You don't clone a colleague, a client, a family member, a celebrity, or anyone else, from photos or videos you found. "It's publicly available" is not permission. A face on a website is not a license to make that face say something. The major providers know this. HeyGen, for example, requires a consent video recorded by the person being cloned, reading the provider's exact consent text, submitted along with the footage, as of the help center we checked for this lesson. If someone else wants an avatar, they record their own consent. Not you on their behalf.

Same for AiRE Estate Academy's own presenters. You'll see me throughout this course as the example. You get no rights to copy my look or my voice, or the owner's. Your spokesperson has to be yours.

Once you've picked a route, write the Spokesperson Brief. It's one page.

Purpose: what are these videos for, who watches, where do they post? "Short educational tips and listing invitations for local sellers and buyers on Facebook and Instagram" is a real answer. "Content" is not.

Presence: three words for tone. Calm, clear, friendly. Energy. Pace. Default framing. And a "never" list: never walks around for no reason, never points at text that isn't there, never over-gestures. You'll thank yourself in Module 5.

Disclosure: how every video identifies AI use. I recommend both: an on-screen "Made with AI" label for the whole video, and a plain line in the caption, like "This video uses an AI avatar of me, made from my own recording." Some platforms have their own AI-content labels too; use them. And remember your normal advertising rules still apply: your brokerage name and any required disclosures belong in AI videos exactly as they do in anything else.

Ownership: whose account is it, who can make videos with this avatar, where are the source footage and consent stored, and what happens if you change brokerages. Write it down now while it's easy.

Then the rights checklist. It's short, and every box matters. For your own likeness: the footage is you, recorded by you; the consent is yours; the voice is yours; you've read the provider's terms. For a fictional character: you have commercial rights to the look and the voice, it doesn't imitate a real person, and it never claims experiences it didn't have.

Your exercise: choose a route, fill in the brief, and complete the checklist. If any box on the checklist can't be ticked, stop there and fix that first. The fanciest video in the world isn't worth a likeness you didn't have the right to use.

Module 2 — Create a Consistent Look

Course: Your AI Video Spokesperson · Status: draft manuscript · Video: not yet recorded

1. Learning objective and required inputs

By the end of this module you can lock a master look (framing, lighting, wardrobe, background) from approved references, and plan two controlled variations that keep your spokesperson recognizable.

You need:

  • Your Spokesperson Brief and Rights Checklist.
  • resources/visual-style-sheet.md.
  • Own-likeness route: a phone or webcam that records at least 1080p at 30 fps, a quiet

room, and soft even light. Fictional route: your licensed character's reference images.

2. Narrated lesson script

Narrator: Eva Vail, AiRE Estate Academy's AI presenter, using owner-approved Eva assets and voice. Target read time about 6–7 minutes.

Think about the agents whose videos you recognize in your feed. You probably know them in half a second, before they say a word. Same framing, same kind of light, often the same jacket. That's not an accident. It's consistency, and it's what makes repeated videos feel like one person instead of a slideshow of strangers.

With AI avatars, consistency matters even more, because small changes compound. Change the background, and the lighting the AI infers changes. Change the jacket and the hair at the same time, and suddenly the avatar in video three looks like a cousin of the one in video one.

So we lock a master look, and we change one thing at a time.

The master look has eight parts on the Style Sheet. Framing: waist up or head and shoulders, centered, eyes in the upper third. Camera height: eye level. Lighting: soft and even, from the front or slightly to one side. Wardrobe: a solid color, no big logos, no busy patterns. Hair and grooming: as you normally look. Accessories: go easy; HeyGen's own guidance says glasses and large accessories can create artifacts. Background: plain and static. And your expression at rest: a slight, natural smile, eyes on the lens.

If you're on the own-likeness route, those choices are made when you film. HeyGen's guidance, as we checked it for this course, is simple: at least 1080p at thirty frames per second, landscape or portrait, bright even light, a simple static background, a quiet room, eye contact with the lens, and one continuous recording with no cuts. It suggests recording at least two minutes, and up to five for the best results. Whatever's in the footage shows up in the avatar, so a cough, an odd movement or an echo will be there forever. Do one or two warm-up takes, then the real one.

If you're on the fictional route, your master look is your reference image set: the same character, same framing, same light, used every time.

Let me show you why one change at a time matters. Here's a master look: navy jacket, grey background, waist up. Here's a controlled variation: same everything, but the background is a softly out-of-focus bookshelf. Still clearly the same person. Now here's an uncontrolled one: new jacket, different hairstyle, outdoors, closer framing. Each change was reasonable on its own. Together, it reads as somebody else.

That's exactly how my own look is managed. My face, hair and framing stay locked, and only the setting changes for different topics. Watch the setting change and the person stay the same. Please don't copy me; copy the method.

Now plan two settings. On the Style Sheet, each planned setting lists what changes and what stays locked. For example: Office, where only the background changes, for introductions. Outdoor, where the background and light change, matched carefully, for listing invitations. Two settings are plenty to start.

Two last things for your brand. Decide where your logo goes and keep it out of the caption area. And keep your brokerage identification consistent from video to video.

Your exercise: fill in the master look, capture or select your master reference frame, and plan two settings. Then put the master and both settings side by side on your phone. If someone who doesn't know you would think they're the same person, you're done.

4. Written walkthrough

Verified October 23, 2026 against HeyGen's help center ("Create your first Digital Twin (Video Avatar) with Avatar IV!").

Own likeness:

  1. Choose your wardrobe: solid color, no large logos or text.
  2. Set up soft, even light facing you; avoid harsh shadows and overexposure.
  3. Choose a plain, static background; quiet room, no other voices.
  4. Set your phone or camera to at least 1080p, 30 fps; landscape (16:9) or portrait (9:16).
  5. Frame yourself per your Style Sheet; camera at eye level.
  6. Do one or two warm-up takes. Then record one continuous take of at least 2 minutes (up to 5 recommended), speaking naturally, looking into the lens, keeping gestures in frame. Don't stop or cut; if you slip, keep going or restart from the beginning.
  7. Save a still frame from the take as your master reference.

Fictional route:

  1. Select or create your character's reference images under a license that covers commercial use; save the license.
  2. Choose one image as the master reference.

Both routes: fill in Style Sheet sections 1–5 and save your master and two planned settings.

Module 3 — Set Up the Voice and Delivery

Course: Your AI Video Spokesperson · Status: draft manuscript · Video: not yet recorded

1. Learning objective and required inputs

By the end of this module you can set up an authorized voice, test pronunciation of the words that matter, adjust pacing and pauses, and preview before rendering.

You need:

  • Your Rights Checklist (voice section ticked).
  • resources/pronunciation-sheet.md.
  • Own-likeness route: your recorded footage (with you speaking) or a clean voice recording.

Fictional route: a licensed voice.

  • Headphones and your phone.

2. Narrated lesson script

Narrator: Eva Vail, AiRE Estate Academy's AI presenter, using owner-approved Eva assets and voice. Target read time about 6 minutes.

Viewers forgive a lot in a short video. A slightly stiff gesture, a plain background. What they don't forgive is a voice that gets your own town wrong. Say "Willow Fork" wrong once and every local viewer knows nobody checked.

So this module is about the voice: getting the right one, and making it say things right.

First, the right one. On the own-likeness route, the voice should be yours. With HeyGen, as of the help center we checked, each video avatar includes voice cloning from your footage, and you can also create a separate voice from a clean audio recording. Either way, it's your voice, recorded by you. One detail from HeyGen's guidance is easy to miss: even if you plan to use a different voice, your filming footage should include you speaking, because that helps lip sync.

On the fictional route, use a stock or licensed voice whose terms allow commercial use, and keep a copy of those terms. Never a voice that imitates a real person. And consent requirements and voice features change, so verify them with the provider before you rely on them.

Now, making it say things right. You'll build a short pronunciation sheet. Seven rows: your name, your brokerage, two towns or neighborhoods, the street names in this week's scripts, numbers, and your call-to-action line.

Write a fifteen-second test script that uses all of them. Something like: "Hi, I'm Sam Reyes with Willow Fork Realty. This is Willow Fork, and I'm on Heron Point Court. If you're thinking about selling this spring, message me." Then preview just that. Not a full video. A short preview costs little or nothing, and it'll catch most problems.

Here's what you'll hear in our example. "Willow Fork," fine. "Heron Point," said "HAIR-on." Not how locals say it. The fix: respell the word for the voice in the script, "Herron Point," and preview again. Your captions keep the correct spelling; only the spoken script gets the respelling. If your provider offers a pronunciation or phonetic setting, that's even better, but check how it works in your account; we didn't rely on one for this lesson.

Numbers deserve their own test. "$612,000" can come out as "six one two thousand" or "six hundred twelve thousand dollars." Write numbers in the script the way you want them spoken, and keep the captions showing the correct figures.

Then pacing. Most AI voices default to a steady, slightly fast read. For short real estate videos, slightly slower than you think usually lands better, with a pause after the hook and another before the call to action. Some providers let you insert pauses or adjust speed; others respond to punctuation, so a period or a line break creates a beat. Try both and listen.

Listen three ways: laptop, headphones, and your phone's speaker at normal volume. The phone speaker is where most of your audience will hear it. If a word is mushy there, fix it.

Your exercise: fill in the pronunciation sheet, write your fifteen-second test script, preview it, fix every problem, and record what you changed. Keep the sheet; you'll reuse it for every video.

Module 4 — Write a Short Script That Holds Attention

Course: Your AI Video Spokesperson · Status: draft manuscript · Video: not yet recorded

1. Learning objective and required inputs

By the end of this module you can write a 15–30 second script with a hook, one useful point, evidence where a claim needs it, and one call to action, and time it by reading it aloud.

You need:

  • resources/short-script-templates.md (three patterns, three fictional worked examples).
  • The fictional listing sheet for 1418 Juniper Lane (from Build Your AI Assistant's

practice data, or the facts repeated below).

  • A timer (your phone).

2. Narrated lesson script

Narrator: Eva Vail, AiRE Estate Academy's AI presenter, using owner-approved Eva assets and voice. Target read time about 7 minutes.

A short video has about three seconds to earn the next three. Most agent videos spend those seconds on "Hey everyone, happy Tuesday, so today I wanted to talk about..." By "talk," the viewer's gone.

Every script in this course has four parts. A hook. One useful point. Evidence, where the point needs it. And one call to action.

The hook is the first line, and it should tell the right viewer this is for them. "Selling in Willow Fork next year?" "Before you list, find your HOA documents." Not "Hi, I'm Sam." Your name can come second.

One useful point. One. Not three tips, not a market overview. One thing the viewer can use. If you have three tips, you have three videos. That's good news, not a problem.

Evidence. If your point includes a fact, the fact needs a source. "Buyers often ask for HOA documents early" is general, careful, and fine without a statistic. "Homes with HOA documents ready sell twenty-two percent faster" needs a real source you can name. If you don't have one, don't say it. AI tools will happily invent a confident percentage. Don't let them.

One call to action. "Message me." "Comment HOA and I'll send the checklist." "Open house Sunday, one to three." Pick one. Two calls to action is zero calls to action.

Now, length. People often say "a hundred and fifty words is a minute," and that's a rough starting point at best. Your spokesperson's pace, the pauses you added in Module 3, and how many numbers you say all change the time. So read every script aloud at the pace you'll use and time it. That's the real length. Aim for fifteen to thirty seconds.

There are three patterns in the templates file, each with a fully worked fictional example.

The introduction. Hook for who it's for, who you are, and a plain AI disclosure: "this is an AI version of me, made from my own recording, so I can post short tips more often. I write and review every one." Then one CTA.

The educational tip. One mistake or question, the one thing to do, careful wording, one CTA. Notice the words "often" and "can." Not "always" and "will." Careful wording is how you stay accurate without a statistic.

The listing invitation. Hook with one true, specific feature from the listing sheet. Then the facts: address, beds, baths, square feet, one more. Then the open house or "message me." Every fact must match the listing sheet. And if it's a real listing, you need the seller's permission to market it, and your MLS and brokerage rules apply.

Now the list of things that never go in a script. Invented testimonials. "My clients always say..." unless it's a real, permitted, verifiable quote. Firsthand experiences the speaker didn't have, which matters a lot for fictional spokespeople. Guaranteed results. Anything about who should live somewhere, school quality claims, or neighborhood "type." And statistics without a source you can name.

You can use AI to draft scripts. Here's the catch: AI drafts tend to break exactly those rules. They open with "Hey there!", they add a third tip, and they invent a percentage. The prompt in this lesson tells it the structure and the rules, and you still read every line against the listing sheet.

Your exercise: write three scripts, one per pattern, for the fictional practice listing. Time each one aloud. Mark every fact and its source. That's your Approved Script Pack.

Everything up to now was preparation: the route, the look, the voice and the script. This is where it becomes a video. We'll make one, start to finish, and then we'll talk about what to do when it comes out wrong, because sometimes it will.

Step one is the avatar. On the own-likeness route in HeyGen, as of the help center we checked for this lesson, you go to the Avatars area, choose New Avatar, and then Clone a Real Person. You can record live or import footage. Use the continuous take you filmed in Module 2. Script timed, facts sourced, disclosure included, voice authorized, pronunciation tested, correct look, correct aspect ratio, and you know what this render costs on your plan.

Step four: render, then review. Watch it four times, each time for one thing.

First pass: identity. Does it look like the master look? Any warping around the eyes, mouth, hands, glasses or jewelry?

Second pass: lip sync. Watch the mouth on words with p, b and m. Does the mouth close?

Third pass: pronunciation. Your name, brokerage, places, numbers.

Fourth pass: unintended changes. Did anything appear that you didn't ask for? A gesture pointing at nothing, an expression that doesn't fit the line, a background shift?

And when it fails. Diagnose before you re-render. If lip sync drifts on one long sentence, shorten the sentence. If one word is wrong, fix the respelling and preview that line. If the face looks off in every video, the problem is the footage, not the script, and no number of re-renders will fix it. Set yourself a rule: two attempts at the same problem, then stop and diagnose. Endless re-rendering is how a free tool turns into an expensive one.

2. Narrated lesson script

Narrator: Eva Vail, AiRE Estate Academy's AI presenter, using owner-approved Eva assets and voice. Target read time about 6–7 minutes.

You've got a talking video. Now let's make it work where people actually watch: on a phone, scrolling, often with the sound off.

Captions first, because for many viewers, captions are the video. Bold, high contrast, big enough to read at arm's length. No more than two lines at a time. And placed in the safe area. Every platform puts its own buttons, profile picture and text over the bottom and right side of a vertical video. If your captions sit there, they're covered. Keep them in the middle band of the frame.

Captions have to match what's spoken. Automatic transcription is a great start and a bad finish. It will spell your brokerage wrong, turn "Heron Point" into "Herring Point," and write "$612" when you said six hundred twelve thousand. Correct every caption by hand against the script and the source. Where your editor allows it, keep the captions as editable text in your project, so a typo next week doesn't mean a new render.

Next, your brand. Logo in a corner that doesn't collide with captions or platform buttons. Brokerage identification as your rules require, in the same place every time. And the "Made with AI" label for the whole video.

B-roll is where movement comes from. A listing photo with a slow push-in. A simple card with the facts: four bedrooms, two and a half baths. Cut to B-roll when it shows something the words describe. Only use photos you have permission to use, which for listings means the seller's authorization and your MLS rules, and match every fact card to the listing sheet.

Music, if you use it, sits under speech. Speech first, always. A good starting point is music well below the voice; then trust your ears. Here's the test that matters: play it on your phone's speaker at a normal volume. If any word is hard to understand, the music is too loud. Use only music licensed for commercial social use.

Now, a common shortcut I want you to skip: merging all three videos into one grid or one long video. Each idea gets its own nine-by-sixteen export. Separate files let you post them on different days, test which hook works, and fix one without touching the others.

And keep a clean version where you can: the video without captions and music. When something changes next month, you'll be glad you have it.

Finally, captions must match the spoken facts. Not more, not less. A caption that adds a detail the voice didn't say, or rounds a number differently, is a mismatch someone will notice. Check every number on screen against the source one more time.

Your exercise: finish all three videos, using the shot and edit plan, and run the captions and audio checklist on each. Name your files clearly. Then watch all three on your phone, sound off, then sound on.

Module 7 — Reuse the Process

Course: Your AI Video Spokesperson · Status: draft manuscript · Video: not yet recorded

1. Learning objective and required inputs

By the end of this module you can save reusable templates, plan controlled variations, batch production, keep a ten-topic content calendar with source checks and approval status, and measure results without expecting guaranteed performance.

  • Your finished videos and all sheets from Modules 1–6.

2. Narrated lesson script

Narrator: Eva Vail, AiRE Estate Academy's AI presenter, using owner-approved Eva assets and voice. Target read time about 6 minutes.

You've made three videos. The fourth one should be faster, and the tenth should feel routine. That only happens if you save what you've built and turn it into a process.

Start with templates. In your video tool, save a project for each pattern: introduction, tip, listing invitation. Same avatar look, same caption style, same logo and AI label positions, same end card. Next time, you duplicate the template and change the script. In your editor, do the same with your caption style and lower thirds. And keep your sheets together: Spokesperson Brief, Style Sheet, Pronunciation Sheet, Script Pack, checklists. That folder is your production kit.

Variations, carefully. You planned two settings in Module 2. Use them. When you want something new, a seasonal background or a different jacket for winter, add it as a planned setting and change one thing. Test it against the master on your phone before you use it for real.

Batching. Instead of making one video every few days, set aside one session to write four scripts, one session to render them, and one to finish them. Batching saves setup time, and it makes you check facts in one focused pass instead of in a hurry.

That's where the content calendar comes in. Ten rows, one per video. For each: the topic, the pattern, the audience, the key fact or point, the source to check, and the date you checked it. Then three status columns: script, render, approval. And the publish date, platform and link once it's live. The approval column matters. Nothing publishes until it says approved, and approved means you watched the finished file, with captions, on your phone.

A template doesn't validate its claims. A saved listing-invitation template with last month's open-house time is how wrong information goes out confidently. Every new video gets the same checks: facts against sources, pronunciation, the four-pass review, captions against speech.

Finally, measuring. Your platforms show views, how long people watched, comments, messages and clicks. Look at them every couple of weeks. Which hooks kept people past three seconds? Which topics brought messages? Use that to choose the next ten topics.

But I want to be clear about what measurement can and can't tell you. These numbers describe what happened. They don't promise what will happen next. No template, hook or posting time guarantees views, leads or listings, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something. Measure, learn, adjust.

Your exercise: save your three templates, fill in a ten-topic calendar using your own market, and write your repeat-production checklist: the steps you'll follow for every new video, in order. That's your reusable production kit.
