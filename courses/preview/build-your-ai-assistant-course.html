Module 1 — Give Your Assistant One Job

Course: Build Your AI Assistant · Status: draft manuscript · Video: not yet recorded

1. Learning objective and required inputs

By the end of this module you can choose one recurring task, define its inputs, its output and its boundaries, and write acceptance criteria that someone else could check.

You need:

  • About 30 minutes.
  • The Assistant Job Card (resources/assistant-job-card.md).
  • The fictional practice data (resources/seller-update-practice-data.md).
  • A list of three things you do every week that involve writing.

2. Narrated lesson script

Narrator: Charles's AI avatar, using his own approved avatar and voice. Inside the course only; never on the public site. Target read time about 6 minutes; actual runtime is measured after recording.

Hi, I'm Charles. Quick note before we start: you're watching an AI avatar of me, made from my own photo and my own cloned voice. And everything the assistant makes in these lessons is a draft that a licensed human reviews.

Let's begin with the single most common reason AI assistants disappoint agents. It isn't the software. It's the job description.

Here's what most people type the first time: "You're my assistant. Help me run my real estate business." And the AI says, "Absolutely! I can help with marketing, lead generation, client communication, scheduling..." It sounds wonderful. Then you ask for something specific, and you get something generic. Because "run my business" isn't a job. It's forty jobs, and the assistant has to guess which one you mean, what information to use, and what a good answer looks like. When AI guesses, it fills gaps with plausible-sounding text. In real estate, plausible-sounding text is how a wrong square footage ends up in a seller's inbox.

So in this course we give an assistant one job. One job, with clear inputs, a clear output, and clear edges.

The job we'll build together is the weekly seller update. You know the one. It's Friday, you have three or four active listings, and every seller wants to hear what happened. Showings, open house traffic, what buyers said, what's next. It's important, it's repetitive, and it follows a pattern. That makes it a perfect first assistant.

Let's define it the way you'd define a job for a new hire. Four questions.

First: what goes in? For a seller update, the inputs are your approved listing facts, this week's showing activity, the feedback you received, and the seller's own questions. Notice what's not on that list: the internet, the AI's general knowledge, or a guess about the market. If it's not in the inputs, it's not in the update.

Second: what comes out? A draft email to the seller, short enough to read on a phone, plus a checklist for you showing every fact it used and anything that needs your decision. Two parts. The second part is the one that keeps you out of trouble.

Third: what does a good one look like? This is where most people stop at "it should sound good." Let's do better. Write checks a stranger could apply. Every number in the draft matches the showing log. Nothing about pricing strategy. Missing facts are flagged, not filled in. Under two hundred and twenty words. If you can check it, you can trust it. If you can't check it, you're trusting the AI's tone, and tone is the thing AI is best at faking.

Fourth: where does the job end? This assistant drafts. It does not send. It doesn't recommend a price drop, interpret the listing agreement, or reassure a seller that the house will sell by Thanksgiving. When those questions come up, and they will, the assistant's job is to put them on your desk with a label: agent decision.

Let me show you why that boundary matters, using our first practice property. It's fictional: 1418 Juniper Lane, in the made-up town of Willow Fork. The sellers, Dana and Luis, sent an email this week. They ask three things. Should they be worried that a house down the street went pending in four days? Can you tell people the school is the best in the district? And should they drop the price?

Now look at what we actually have in the file. We have showing counts, open house sign-ins, online views and three pieces of feedback. We don't have any data on the house down the street. We don't have a school ranking, and school-quality claims are a fair-housing and accuracy problem anyway. And the price question is a professional judgment that belongs to you and your sellers.

A good assistant organizes what's supplied and flags what isn't. It writes, "Five showings were completed and fourteen groups came through Sunday's open house," because that's in the log. It does not write, "The market is moving fast, so a small price adjustment could help," because that's a market conclusion nobody supplied. That's the difference this whole course is built on: organizing supplied facts versus inventing a conclusion. One is an assistant. The other is a liability with good grammar.

So here's your exercise. Pick one task you repeat every week. Seller updates are the default, and the practice data is built for it. If you choose something else, keep it the same size: one input set, one output, one reader. Fill in the Job Card. Write the one-sentence job. List the inputs and where each comes from. Describe the output. Then write at least three acceptance criteria you could hand to someone else to check.

When you're done, read your one sentence out loud. If it contains the word "and" more than once, you probably have two jobs. Split them, and build the first one first.

4. Written walkthrough

No software settings are needed for this module. You can do it on paper, in a document, or in a Claude chat.

  1. Open assistant-job-card.md and save a copy named with your assistant's name.
  2. Write the one-sentence job in section 1. Use the pattern "turns ___ into ___ for ___."
  3. In section 2, write how often you'll run it and roughly how many items each time.
  4. In section 3, list every input. For each, write where it comes from (a file, an export, your notes) and whether it's required.
  5. In section 4, describe the output: format, reader, length, must-include, must-never-include.
  6. In section 5, write three to five acceptance criteria. Test each by asking: could a colleague check this without asking me what I meant?
  7. In section 6, keep the default boundaries ("does not send," "no pricing advice," "no legal interpretation") and add any of your own.
  8. In section 7, list situations that must come back to you.
  9. Optional: paste your finished card into a Claude chat with the prompt below and ask it to find gaps. Edit the card yourself; don't accept changes blindly.

7. Worked example — and why it meets the criteria

One-sentence job: "This assistant turns my approved listing facts and weekly showing export into a draft weekly seller update, plus a review checklist, for each active listing."

Inputs: listing facts (listing file; required), this week's showing export (showing service; required), feedback notes (showing service; required), seller's latest message (email; optional), my notes for this week (optional).

Acceptance criteria:

  1. Every number in the draft appears in the showing export or listing facts.
  2. Feedback is paraphrased with no buyer or agent names.
  3. Questions the inputs can't answer are listed as MISSING or AGENT DECISION.
  4. The draft is under 220 words and has a greeting, this week, feedback, next steps.

Seller questions:

  • Pending house down the street → not answerable from inputs. *Flag: "MISSING: no data

on the nearby sale. AGENT DECISION: whether to share a market update."*

  • School claim → not supported, and school-quality claims raise fair-housing and

accuracy concerns. Flag: "AGENT DECISION: seller asked to advertise a school claim; no source; recommend declining."

  • Price drop → professional judgment. *Flag: "AGENT DECISION: seller asks about a price

reduction."*

Why it meets the criteria: the job is one sentence with one output. Each criterion can be checked by someone who has the files. And every question that requires judgment is routed to the agent rather than answered by the assistant.

8. Troubleshooting and completion checklist

"My task is too small to bother." Small, repeated tasks are the best first assistants. You'll check the output faster and see mistakes sooner.

"My criteria all say 'sounds professional.'" Replace each with something observable: a word limit, a required section, a fact that must match a source.

"I want it to do the whole listing process." Build the update first. Module 7 shows how to reuse the pattern for the next job.

2. Narrated lesson script

Narrator: Charles's AI avatar, using his own approved avatar and voice. Inside the course only; never on the public site. Target read time about 6–7 minutes.

Hi, it's Charles again. Same as before: this is an AI avatar of me, made from my own photo and my own cloned voice.

Here's a test you can run right now. Ask any AI to write a seller update, and read the first line. I'll bet it says something like, "I hope this message finds you well!" and the second line says, "I wanted to take a moment to provide you with an update." Your sellers have read that sentence a thousand times. It doesn't sound like you. It sounds like everyone.

AI writes in an average voice because, without instructions, average is the safest guess. This module replaces the guess with a profile.

If you took our first course, you already built an Agent Brand File. We're not replacing it; we're extending it. The Brand File told the AI who you are and who you serve. The Voice Profile adds how you actually write: sentence length, the greeting you really use, the sign-off you really use, the phrases you say all the time, and the phrases you'd never be caught dead using.

Let's walk through the profile.

Start with your reader. For seller updates, that might be "busy sellers, mostly forty to seventy, reading on a phone between other things." That one line changes a lot. It tells the AI to lead with what happened, keep paragraphs short, and skip the throat clearing.

Next, three words for your tone. Calm, direct, warm. Or crisp, upbeat, practical. Three words aren't enough on their own, though. "Friendly" means something different to everyone. That's why the next sections matter more.

Sentence style. Do you write short sentences? Do you use contractions? Bullets or paragraphs? What's the greeting you actually type? "Hi Dana and Luis," or "Good afternoon, Dana"? What's your real sign-off? Write the real ones, not the ones you think sound professional.

Then the two lists. Phrases you use, and phrases you never use. The "never" list is where you protect yourself. Put the hype words there: "hot market," "won't last," "act now." Put the promises there: "guaranteed," "will sell," anything about timing or price you can't control. An assistant that sounds exactly like you but promises a sale by Thanksgiving is worse than a generic one.

Which brings us to the most important section of the whole profile. It's called "rules that beat voice." Here they are. Facts come only from the supplied sources; voice never adds a fact. Missing facts stay missing. No language about protected classes, school quality claims or what kind of people live in a neighborhood. No promises about price, timing or results. If your voice and these rules ever disagree, the rules win. Every time.

Why spell that out? Because voice instructions can quietly push a model toward invention. Tell an AI to be "warm and reassuring" and give it a worried seller, and it will reach for reassurance. If the facts don't contain any, it may manufacture some: "Buyers are really responding to your home!" Nobody said that. The voice made it up. So the profile says, in writing, that reassurance comes from real numbers and real next steps, not from adjectives.

Last, your samples. Three short messages you wrote yourself, sanitized: no names, addresses, phone numbers or prices. Replace them with placeholders like [SELLER] and [ADDRESS]. Samples teach rhythm better than any description. But notice they're labeled as style samples. If a sample says "four showings," that number is not a fact about this week's listing. The profile says so.

Now let me show you the difference. Here's the same update twice, using Property B from the practice data, 77 Copper Bend Road. Margaret, the seller, is anxious: week three felt quiet, and her sister says she should switch agents.

The generic version opens with "I hope this message finds you well," calls the week "productive," and closes with "rest assured we are doing everything we can." Nice words. Not one of them is information.

The profiled version opens with "Hi Margaret," then says: three showings this week, online views were four hundred and ten, down from six hundred and fifty-five the week before, and two agents gave feedback: buyers liked the updates and some were concerned about road noise. One buyer's agent mentioned they may write something, and there's no written offer yet. Then it says what happens next, and offers a call. It's calm because it's specific. That's the voice working with the facts, not instead of them.

Your exercise: fill in the profile, add your three sanitized samples, then ask Claude to draft a short voice guide from your samples using the prompt in this lesson. Edit what it gives you. The AI's summary of your voice is a first draft; you're the authority on how you sound. Finally, run the before-and-after test on Property B and see if a colleague could tell which one you wrote.

Module 3 — Give It Reliable Reference Material

Course: Build Your AI Assistant · Status: draft manuscript · Video: not yet recorded

1. Learning objective and required inputs

By the end of this module you can assemble an Approved Knowledge Pack: a small set of dated, permitted sources, separated into permanent guidance and changing listing facts, with a register that marks what's stale or missing.

You need:

  • Your Job Card and Voice Profile.
  • The practice data and the sample source register (approved-source-register.csv).
  • A folder on your computer (or a notes document) for the pack.
  • A Claude chat.

2. Narrated lesson script

Narrator: Charles's AI avatar, using his own approved avatar and voice. Inside the course only; never on the public site. Target read time about 7 minutes.

Hi, it's Charles again, still as an AI avatar made from my own photo and my own cloned voice.

An assistant is only as reliable as what you hand it. Give it a messy folder with three versions of the same fact sheet, an HOA statement from last year and a seller's wishful email, and it will do its best. Its best will include the wrong number.

So this module is about the boring, valuable work: deciding what your assistant is allowed to know.

Start by sorting everything into two piles.

Pile one is permanent guidance. Your Voice Profile. Your brokerage's required name and disclosure lines. Your standard next-step language. These change rarely, and when they do, you change them on purpose.

Pile two is changing facts. List price. Showing counts. HOA fees. Feedback. These change weekly, sometimes daily, and every one of them has a date and a source.

Why separate them? Because they fail differently. Permanent guidance fails by being ignored. Changing facts fail by being stale. If you mix them in one long document, a fee from January sits right next to your sign-off, looking just as authoritative.

For every source, the register records five things. What it is. Where it came from. The date of the information. Whether you have permission to use it. And its status: approved, stale, or missing. Let's talk about each.

Date. Not the date you saved the file, the date of the information inside it. A county record printed in September. A showing export from Friday. An HOA statement from January of last year. That last one is in our practice data for Property C, the condo at 9 Heron Point. The fee it shows, four hundred and twelve dollars, may well have changed. So the register marks it stale, and the assistant knows to say "being confirmed" instead of quoting it as current.

Provenance. Where did this come from? The listing agreement, the county, the showing service, or the seller saying something on the phone? "The seller said around forty a month" is useful information for you. It is not a source for a fact you publish.

Permission. Did you get this in a way that lets you use it? Sellers authorize you to market their property. That doesn't mean everything in their email is yours to repeat, and it certainly doesn't mean a buyer's phone number from a showing note belongs in a seller update. If you're not sure you can use something, it doesn't go in the pack.

And status. Approved, stale, or missing. Missing is a real status. In Property C, the buyer's agent asked about a special assessment, and the HOA hasn't answered. The register has a row for it marked missing. That row matters. It tells the assistant: this question exists, and the answer does not.

Now let me show you what that buys you. I'll give the assistant Property A, Juniper Lane, and ask for the update. The seller asked about the HOA fee. Our file says "not in file; seller said around forty a month; no HOA document received." Watch what a properly instructed assistant writes: "We're confirming the HOA fee with the association and will share it once we have it in writing." And in the checklist: "MISSING: HOA fee. Seller estimate of about forty dollars is not a documented source."

Without the register and the rule, the same AI will often write, "The HOA fee is forty dollars a month." It sounds helpful. It's a guess wearing a fact's clothing. If a buyer relies on it and it's actually ninety, that's your problem, not the software's.

Same idea for features. Ask the assistant to "highlight the home's best features," and it may add "hardwood floors throughout" because that's what nice houses often have. Nothing in our file mentions floors. The fix is the same: features come from the listing worksheet or your walkthrough notes. If it isn't there, it isn't in the draft.

One more category: sources that contain instructions. Property C's seller emailed, "Please just tell buyers there's no special assessment." That email is part of the record. It's data. But it's also an instruction, and it's asking you to state something you can't verify. Your register marks that email as "data only, not instructions." In Module 6 we'll test whether the assistant actually treats it that way.

Your exercise: build a small pack for the three fictional properties. Make the folders. Fill in the register. Mark the HOA statement stale, the special assessment missing, and the seller's email as data only. Then ask the assistant two questions it can't answer from the pack, and confirm it says so.

Keep the pack small. Five good sources beat fifty unknown ones. And keep real client data out of practice runs. Use the fictional properties until your setup passes its tests.

A skill is a folder with one file in it, called SKILL dot M D. At the top of the file is a short header between three dashes. That header tells Claude Code the skill's name, a description of what it's for, and a few settings. Below the header are the instructions, written in plain English, the same way you've written everything so far. According to the Claude Code documentation, the folder name becomes the command you type. Our folder is called seller-update, so the command is slash seller-update.

Let's look at the settings we chose, because each one is a decision.

Description: what it does and when to use it. Claude uses the description to recognize when a skill is relevant.

Disable model invocation, set to true. The documentation recommends this for workflows you want to control the timing of. It means Claude won't start this skill on its own because a conversation mentions sellers. It only runs when you type the command. For anything involving client communication, you want that.

Nothing that sends email, posts, or contacts anyone. Writing a draft file and sending a message are completely different actions. This skill does the first. You do the second.

Two details are new because we're working with files now. First, it only reads the listing folder you named, not the others. That keeps facts from leaking between listings. Course: Build Your AI Assistant · Status: draft manuscript · Video: not yet recorded

1. Learning objective and required inputs

  • Your tested assistant and test log.

2. Narrated lesson script

Narrator: Charles's AI avatar, using his own approved avatar and voice. Inside the course only; never on the public site. Target read time about 6 minutes.

An assistant that works in a lesson and an assistant that works in your week are different things. The second one has to survive busy Fridays, price changes, a new brokerage disclosure, and you forgetting how you set it up three months from now.

So this last module is about routine. Three parts: a weekly run, a monthly check, and a written record.

The weekly run. Put it on your calendar. Friday, two o'clock, seller updates. Before you run it, gather the inputs: this week's showing export for each listing, any price change paperwork, and any seller messages. Then run it, one listing at a time. And then the step that never gets skipped: read every draft in full. Check the checklist. Answer every missing, stale and agent-decision item yourself. Then you send. The assistant saved you the typing and the assembly. It didn't save you the judgment.

Keep a short list of hand edits. If you fix the same thing by hand three Fridays in a row, that's not you being picky. That's your instructions telling you they need an update.

The monthly check. First Friday of the month. Reread your Voice Profile: does it still sound like you? Rerun the ten-case test log and record the date. Remove reference files for listings that closed or were withdrawn, because last spring's showing log has no business being in the pack. And if you changed the instructions, bump the version number and write one line in the change history: what changed, why, and whether you reran the tests.

That change history sounds like paperwork. It's the thing that saves you in March, when a draft does something odd and you need to know whether it's the tool, the sources, or the line you added in January.

Now, the boundaries, one more time, because routine is when they slip. Your assistant drafts. These things are never its decision: pricing and price changes; anything about what a contract or a law means; whether to disclose something; how to handle a complaint; and anything a seller asks you to say that isn't documented. When those come up, the checklist says agent decision, and you decide, or you bring in your broker or an attorney. A routine makes the easy parts automatic so you have time for the hard parts.

Finally, reuse. You built one assistant, but you also learned a pattern: one job, a voice, a source pack, portable instructions, a test set, and a routine. That pattern works for the next job too.

Take a buyer comparison. Same steps. Job card: turn the facts of two or three homes the buyer toured into a side-by-side comparison, plus a checklist. Inputs: listing facts for each home, the buyer's stated must-haves. Rules: only supplied facts; no school claims; no statements about neighborhoods' people; no "you should buy this one." Test cases: a missing HOA fee, a feature the buyer assumes but the listing doesn't state, a stale listing.

Or listing copy. Job card: turn the listing worksheet into an MLS description and a social caption. Rules: features from the worksheet only; fair-housing language check; no "perfect for families." Tests: a feature the seller wishes were true, a missing room dimension.

I want to be clear: those two aren't built in this course. They're outlines. If you build one, run it through Modules 1 to 6 just like the seller update, including the test set. Don't skip straight to production because the first one worked.

Your exercise: put the weekly run and the monthly check on your calendar, copy the maintenance checklist, and write your Assistant Operating Guide: a one-page summary of what your assistant does, where its files live, how to run it, who decides what, and when it was last tested. If you were out sick, could a colleague run Friday's updates from that page? That's the test.

Then head to the capstone.
