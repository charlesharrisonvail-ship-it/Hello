# Module 1 — Choose Who Appears On Camera

**Course:** Your AI Video Spokesperson · **Status:** draft manuscript · **Video:** not yet recorded

## 1. Learning objective and required inputs

**By the end of this module you can** choose between an own-likeness avatar and a
fictional spokesperson, decide appearance, tone, audience and disclosure, and document
the permissions you have.

**You need:**
- `resources/spokesperson-brief.md`
- `resources/likeness-and-voice-rights-checklist.md`
- Your brokerage's advertising policy (ask your broker if you don't have it).

## 2. Narrated lesson script

*Narrator: AiRE, the AI Instructor. Target read time about 6 minutes. Where the script
calls for an Eva Vail example, use only owner-approved Eva assets and voice; otherwise
cut the example.*

Hi, I'm AiRE. I'm an AI instructor, and this whole course is about making videos with AI
on-camera presenters, so let's start with the honest part.

Before you pick a tool, a background or a script, you decide one thing: who appears on
camera. There are two routes, and only two.

Route one: your own likeness. You record yourself, and the provider builds an avatar of
you, sometimes called a digital twin, along with a clone of your voice. Your videos then
feature a version of you saying scripts you write. People who know you will recognize
you. That's the point, and it's also the responsibility: everything that avatar says,
you said.

Route two: a fictional spokesperson. A character who isn't a real person, created with a
tool whose terms let you use it commercially, or licensed from a provider's stock
library. A fictional spokesperson can be consistent and professional. But they can't
claim to be a licensed agent, they can't say "when I sold my own home," and every video
has to make clear they're AI-generated.

There is no route three. You don't clone a colleague, a client, a family member, a
celebrity, or anyone else, from photos or videos you found. "It's publicly available" is
not permission. A face on a website is not a license to make that face say something.
The major providers know this. HeyGen, for example, requires a consent video recorded by
the person being cloned, reading the provider's exact consent text, submitted along with
the footage, as of the help center we checked for this lesson. If someone else wants an
avatar, they record their own consent. Not you on their behalf.

Same for AiRE Estate's own presenters. You'll see Eva Vail and me in this course as
examples. You get no rights to copy Eva's look or voice, mine, or the owner's. Your
spokesperson has to be yours.

Once you've picked a route, write the Spokesperson Brief. It's one page.

Purpose: what are these videos for, who watches, where do they post? "Short educational
tips and listing invitations for local sellers and buyers on Facebook and Instagram" is a
real answer. "Content" is not.

Presence: three words for tone. Calm, clear, friendly. Energy. Pace. Default framing. And
a "never" list: never walks around for no reason, never points at text that isn't there,
never over-gestures. You'll thank yourself in Module 5.

Disclosure: how every video identifies AI use. I recommend both: an on-screen "Made with
AI" label for the whole video, and a plain line in the caption, like "This video uses an
AI avatar of me, made from my own recording." Some platforms have their own AI-content
labels too; use them. And remember your normal advertising rules still apply: your
brokerage name and any required disclosures belong in AI videos exactly as they do in
anything else.

Ownership: whose account is it, who can make videos with this avatar, where are the
source footage and consent stored, and what happens if you change brokerages. Write it
down now while it's easy.

Then the rights checklist. It's short, and every box matters. For your own likeness: the
footage is you, recorded by you; the consent is yours; the voice is yours; you've read
the provider's terms. For a fictional character: you have commercial rights to the look
and the voice, it doesn't imitate a real person, and it never claims experiences it
didn't have.

Your exercise: choose a route, fill in the brief, and complete the checklist. If any box
on the checklist can't be ticked, stop there and fix that first. The fanciest video in
the world isn't worth a likeness you didn't have the right to use.

## 4. Written walkthrough

**Verified October 23, 2026** against HeyGen's help center ("Recording your Consent
Video," "Digital Twin FAQ").

1. Read both routes in the brief and tick one.
2. Fill in Purpose (section 2) with platform names and audience.
3. Fill in Presence (section 3), including the "never" list.
4. Write your disclosure line (section 4). Check your brokerage's policy and your state's
   advertising rules for required identification.
5. Fill in Ownership (section 5).
6. Complete the matching section (A or B) of the Rights Checklist, then section C.
7. If you chose your own likeness: note that the provider will require you to record a
   consent video, reading its exact text, at the time you submit footage (HeyGen: under
   30 seconds; MP4, MOV or WebM).
8. Keep both documents with your source footage records.

## 6. Practice exercise (fictional inputs)

Fictional agent Sam Reyes, Willow Fork Realty, wants weekly 20-second videos. Sam is
camera-shy but comfortable recording one five-minute clip.

1. Which route fits Sam? Why?
2. Write Sam's disclosure line.
3. Sam's business partner, Alex, says, "Just use my LinkedIn video to make an avatar of
   me too." What's the right response?

## 7. Worked example — and why it meets the criteria

1. **Own likeness.** Sam can record one clip and consent personally; viewers will
   recognize Sam, which helps local trust.
2. **Disclosure:** on-screen "Made with AI" for the full video, plus caption: "This video
   uses an AI avatar of me, made from my own recording. I write and review every one."
3. **Alex:** Alex must record their own footage and their own consent video with the
   provider, and Sam and Alex should agree in writing on how the avatar is used. A
   LinkedIn video is not permission, and the provider's consent step requires the person
   themselves.

**Why it meets the criteria:** the route matches who's consenting; disclosure is visible
and plain; the third-party request is handled with consent from the person, not
assumptions.

## 8. Troubleshooting and completion checklist

**"I want a spokesperson who looks like a famous actor."** No. Use your own likeness or a
clearly fictional, licensed character.

**"My brokerage hasn't said anything about AI video."** Ask. Get the answer in writing.

**Completion checklist**
- [ ] Route chosen.
- [ ] Brief sections 1–5 complete.
- [ ] Disclosure line written.
- [ ] Rights Checklist complete with no unticked box.

## 9. Module check

### Q1. Which are the two routes for an AI spokesperson in this course?
- A) Your own likeness, or a properly licensed fictional spokesperson.
- B) Your own likeness, or a colleague's.
- C) A celebrity look-alike, or a stock photo of a real person.
- D) Any face you find online.
**Answer:** A — Only you, or a fictional character you have commercial rights to.

### Q2. A real person's photos are publicly available online. Does that give you permission to clone them?
- A) Yes, if the photos are public.
- B) Yes, if you credit them.
- C) No; public availability isn't permission, and providers require the person's own consent.
- D) Only for non-commercial videos.
**Answer:** C — Consent comes from the person, not from where the image is posted.

### Q3. Per HeyGen's help center (checked Oct. 23, 2026), who records the consent video for a digital twin?
- A) The account owner, for anyone.
- B) The person whose avatar is being created.
- C) The provider's staff.
- D) No consent video is needed.
**Answer:** B — The consent video must feature the same person as the footage.

### Q4. A fictional spokesperson says, "When I sold my first home..." What's wrong?
- A) Nothing.
- B) It's a firsthand experience the character didn't have.
- C) It's too short.
- D) Fictional characters can't speak.
**Answer:** B — Fictional spokespeople don't claim real experiences or licenses.

### Q5. Which disclosure approach does this module recommend?
- A) None; viewers can tell.
- B) An on-screen "Made with AI" label plus a plain line in the caption.
- C) A disclosure only in your profile bio.
- D) Mention it once a year.
**Answer:** B — Visible and plain, on every video.

### Q6. Can students use Eva Vail as their spokesperson?
- A) Yes, she's in the course.
- B) Yes, with a different name.
- C) No; the course grants no rights to copy AiRE Estate's presenters, their voices, or its owner.
- D) Only on Instagram.
**Answer:** C — Your spokesperson must be yours.

### Q7. Do your brokerage's advertising rules apply to AI avatar videos?
- A) No, AI videos are exempt.
- B) Yes; brokerage identification and required disclosures still apply.
- C) Only if the video is longer than a minute.
- D) Only for listings over $1 million.
**Answer:** B — AI is a production method, not an exemption.
