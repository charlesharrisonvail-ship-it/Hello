Think about the agents whose videos you recognize in your feed. You probably know them in half a second, before they say a word. Same framing, same kind of light, often the same jacket. That's not an accident. It's consistency, and it's what makes repeated videos feel like one person instead of a slideshow of strangers.

With AI avatars, consistency matters even more, because small changes compound. Change the background, and the lighting the AI infers changes. Change the jacket and the hair at the same time, and suddenly the avatar in video three looks like a cousin of the one in video one.

So we lock a master look, and we change one thing at a time.

The master look has eight parts on the Style Sheet. Framing: waist up or head and shoulders, centered, eyes in the upper third. Camera height: eye level. Lighting: soft and even, from the front or slightly to one side. Wardrobe: a solid color, no big logos, no busy patterns. Hair and grooming: as you normally look. Accessories: go easy; HeyGen's own guidance says glasses and large accessories can create artifacts. Background: plain and static. And your expression at rest: a slight, natural smile, eyes on the lens.

If you're on the own-likeness route, those choices are made when you film. HeyGen's guidance, as we checked it for this course, is simple: at least 1080p at thirty frames per second, landscape or portrait, bright even light, a simple static background, a quiet room, eye contact with the lens, and one continuous recording with no cuts. It suggests recording at least two minutes, and up to five for the best results. Whatever's in the footage shows up in the avatar, so a cough, an odd movement or an echo will be there forever. Do one or two warm-up takes, then the real one.

If you're on the fictional route, your master look is your reference image set: the same character, same framing, same light, used every time.

Let me show you why one change at a time matters. Here's a master look: navy jacket, grey background, waist up. Here's a controlled variation: same everything, but the background is a softly out-of-focus bookshelf. Still clearly the same person. Now here's an uncontrolled one: new jacket, different hairstyle, outdoors, closer framing. Each change was reasonable on its own. Together, it reads as somebody else.

That's exactly how my own look is managed. My face, hair and framing stay locked, and only the setting changes for different topics. Watch the setting change and the person stay the same. Please don't copy me; copy the method.

Now plan two settings. On the Style Sheet, each planned setting lists what changes and what stays locked. For example: Office, where only the background changes, for introductions. Outdoor, where the background and light change, matched carefully, for listing invitations. Two settings are plenty to start.

Two last things for your brand. Decide where your logo goes and keep it out of the caption area. And keep your brokerage identification consistent from video to video.

Your exercise: fill in the master look, capture or select your master reference frame, and plan two settings. Then put the master and both settings side by side on your phone. If someone who doesn't know you would think they're the same person, you're done.
