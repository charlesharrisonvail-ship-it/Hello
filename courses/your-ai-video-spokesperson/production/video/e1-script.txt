Hi, I'm Eva Vail. I'm an AI presenter for Air Estate Academy. I'm not a real person, and this whole course is about making videos with AI on-camera presenters like me, so let's start with the honest part.

Before you pick a tool, a background or a script, you decide one thing: who appears on camera. There are two routes, and only two.

Route one: your own likeness. You record yourself, and the provider builds an avatar of you, sometimes called a digital twin, along with a clone of your voice. Your videos then feature a version of you saying scripts you write. People who know you will recognize you. That's the point, and it's also the responsibility: everything that avatar says, you said.

Route two: a fictional spokesperson. A character who isn't a real person, created with a tool whose terms let you use it commercially, or licensed from a provider's stock library. A fictional spokesperson can be consistent and professional. But they can't claim to be a licensed agent, they can't say "when I sold my own home," and every video has to make clear they're AI-generated.

There is no route three. You don't clone a colleague, a client, a family member, a celebrity, or anyone else, from photos or videos you found. "It's publicly available" is not permission. A face on a website is not a license to make that face say something. The major providers know this. HeyGen, for example, requires a consent video recorded by the person being cloned, reading the provider's exact consent text, submitted along with the footage, as of the help center we checked for this lesson. If someone else wants an avatar, they record their own consent. Not you on their behalf.

Same for Air Estate Academy's own presenters. You'll see me throughout this course as the example. You get no rights to copy my look or my voice, or the owner's. Your spokesperson has to be yours.

Once you've picked a route, write the Spokesperson Brief. It's one page.

Purpose: what are these videos for, who watches, where do they post? "Short educational tips and listing invitations for local sellers and buyers on Facebook and Instagram" is a real answer. "Content" is not.

Presence: three words for tone. Calm, clear, friendly. Energy. Pace. Default framing. And a "never" list: never walks around for no reason, never points at text that isn't there, never over-gestures. You'll thank yourself in Module 5.

Disclosure: how every video identifies AI use. I recommend both: an on-screen "Made with AI" label for the whole video, and a plain line in the caption, like "This video uses an AI avatar of me, made from my own recording." Some platforms have their own AI-content labels too; use them. And remember your normal advertising rules still apply: your brokerage name and any required disclosures belong in AI videos exactly as they do in anything else.

Ownership: whose account is it, who can make videos with this avatar, where are the source footage and consent stored, and what happens if you change brokerages. Write it down now while it's easy.

Then the rights checklist. It's short, and every box matters. For your own likeness: the footage is you, recorded by you; the consent is yours; the voice is yours; you've read the provider's terms. For a fictional character: you have commercial rights to the look and the voice, it doesn't imitate a real person, and it never claims experiences it didn't have.

Your exercise: choose a route, fill in the brief, and complete the checklist. If any box on the checklist can't be ticked, stop there and fix that first. The fanciest video in the world isn't worth a likeness you didn't have the right to use.
